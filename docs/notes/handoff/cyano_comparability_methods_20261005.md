## Bottom line

The literature supports one ordering of evidence. Agreement between two studies on a condition **response** (each study's treatment-versus-own-control log fold changes) is stronger evidence of comparable biology than agreement on absolute expression **levels**, and both are interpretable only against a **yardstick** formed by each dataset's own replicate agreement. Whole-distribution similarity (KS statistics, quantile overlap) is close to uninformative after normalization, because the common normalizations either force distributions to match or rest on assumptions that hide global differences. The recommended per-pair evidence panel therefore has six computed items (shared-feature coverage, replicate yardstick, level concordance placed on the yardstick, an agreement plot, response concordance where both studies have a control, and a joint embedding with known labels), plus one item displayed only as a units sanity check (marginal distributions). Each item is a guide for the human reviewer, not a gate. Package D's finding that metadata incompleteness, not biology, drives the current verdicts (`docs/notes/handoff/RET_claude-science-session__20261004.md`) is consistent with this framing: the data-derived panel supplies evidence that the metadata cannot.

## Survey of methods

### Level concordance and its yardstick

Gene-wise correlation of expression levels on shared genes is the most common cross-study comparison, but the literature treats correlation as a measure of association rather than agreement. [Bland and Altman 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2886%2990837-8) (abstract only) argued that correlation coefficients are misleading when the question is whether two measurements agree, and proposed a difference-versus-mean analysis instead; [Lin 1989](https://doi.org/10.2307/2532051) (abstract only) defined the concordance correlation coefficient as the correlation of paired readings that fall on the 45° line, which penalises location and scale shifts that Pearson and Spearman ignore. For data carrying only relative information, such as TPM or any composition-constrained unit, [Lovell et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004075) showed that correlations can be spurious because of the common divisor and proposed proportionality as an alternative. Rank-based measures remove scale differences between units but not these compositional effects.

A between-study correlation has no meaning in isolation; the comparison the literature makes is against replicate agreement. The irreproducible discovery rate of [Li et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1214/11-AOAS466) (abstract only) is built on paired replicate ranks and permits an arbitrary scale for each replicate, which makes rank agreement between replicates the natural reference. The proteomic batch-effect protocol of [Čuklina et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.15252/msb.202110240) makes the same comparison operational: its quality-control step compares sample correlations within and between batches and pays special attention to replicate correlation. The only *S. elongatus* compendium found, the iModulon analysis of [Yuan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410492121), filtered samples whose correlation with their biological replicates was R² < 0.91 and discarded samples lacking replicates, retaining 300 of 424 profiles across 158 conditions. That threshold is a compendium inclusion rule, not a comparability criterion, but it shows the replicate yardstick in use on this species.

Multi-laboratory benchmarks fix the expected size of the gap between within-study and between-study agreement. [Irizarry et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth756) (abstract only) found relatively large differences between laboratories using the same platform on identical RNA, with the best-performing laboratories agreeing well; the [MAQC Consortium 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt1239) reported intraplatform consistency across test sites and high interplatform concordance in the genes called differentially expressed. MAQC compared standardized reference RNA, principally the strongly separated A-versus-B samples; its discussion cautions that weaker biological contrasts need not achieve the same consistency. Gene filtering, quality exclusions and platform-specific detection also condition that result. On identical T-cell samples, RNA-seq and Affymetrix profiles correlated at r = 0.88–0.90 ([Zhao et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0078644)). A between-study level correlation below the within-study replicate range is therefore expected even when the biology is identical, and its distance below that range, not its absolute value, is what the reviewer should read.

### Response concordance

Agreement on relative change is consistently more reproducible than agreement on absolute level. The SEQC/MAQC-III project found relative expression accurate and reproducible across sites and platforms when filters were applied, whereas neither RNA-seq nor microarrays gave accurate absolute measurements ([SEQC/MAQC-III Consortium 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.2957)). [Parmigiani et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-03-0490) (abstract only) compared lung-cancer studies on different platforms through the reproducibility of gene-wise t statistics and integrative correlations, explicitly avoiding direct assimilation of expression values across platforms; [Garrett-Mayer et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxm033) (abstract only) extended these tools and found that the subset of genes with reliable cross-study agreement varies with platform, tissue and population.

Threshold-free comparison of two ranked response lists is available through rank-rank hypergeometric overlap ([Plaisier et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkq636)), whose output is a map of the strength, pattern and bounds of correlation between two expression profiles; [Cahill et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-27903-2) corrected it to detect same-direction and opposite-direction overlap separately. Two qualifications apply. Cross-platform agreement on differentially expressed genes scales with treatment effect size: across 27 chemical treatments it ranged from 60% for a strong perturbation to 25% for a weak one ([Wang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.3001)). Low response concordance is thus weak evidence against comparability when both responses are small. Conversely, high response concordance can be nonspecific: across more than 600 studies, a gene's prior probability of differential expression alone predicted hit lists with a mean AUROC near 0.8, indicating that much replicated differential expression reflects recurrent generic processes ([Crow et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1802973116)).

### Batch effects versus condition effects

The literature separates batch from condition by examining structure against known labels, and warns that this separation fails when the two are confounded. [Leek et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg2825) describe a bladder-cancer study whose hierarchical clustering separated disease groups that were strongly confounded with processing date. The Mouse ENCODE result that human and mouse tissues cluster by species on the first three principal components of the broad tissue analysis ([Lin et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1413624111)) was reanalysed by [Gilad and Mizrahi-Man 2015](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.6536.1), who showed that sequencing flowcell and lane were confounded with species and that, after accounting for batch, samples clustered by tissue. Lin also reports tissue-dominated structure on PCs 4–6 and on PCs 1–3 when restricting the analysis to selected tissue types, so the original clustering result is sensitive to the analysis scope.

Unsupervised PCA can miss a batch effect that is not the largest source of variance; guided PCA supplies a permutation test using specified batch indicators ([Reese et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btt480)). Reese requires known potential batch labels; its simulations generated phenotype independently of batch and its case studies measured copy-number variation. For this project, association with study labels would not distinguish technical batch from biology when study and condition coincide, and that validation supplies no RNA-seq pair-comparability cutoff. Where a design includes more than one condition per study, linear mixed models apportion each gene's variance among study, condition and residual as a fraction of variation explained ([Hoffman and Schadt 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12859-016-1323-z)); surrogate variable analysis addresses unmeasured heterogeneity ([Leek and Storey 2007](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.0030161)).

Adjustment methods carry a specific risk for this project. ComBat estimates batch location and scale by empirical Bayes and is robust in small batches ([Johnson et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxj037), abstract only), and ComBat-seq applies the same idea to counts through a negative binomial model ([Zhang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1093/nargab/lqaa078)). When groups are unevenly distributed across batches, however, batch adjustment usually deflates true group differences, and preserving group as a covariate in an unbalanced design can induce false group differences ([Nygaard et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxv027)). Biological heterogeneity can be mistaken for batch and removed ([Goh et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tibtech.2017.02.012), abstract only), and data in which biological groups are completely confounded with batches can be biased beyond repair ([Čuklina et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.15252/msb.202110240)). In the cross-study setting nearly every study is its own batch and usually its own condition, so batch correction cannot be used to establish comparability; the comparability evidence should be computed on uncorrected, rank-transformed data.

### Cross-platform and cross-unit agreement

Platform differences are real but usually smaller than treatment effects. In a mouse hypertension model, treatment had a far greater effect than platform for more than 90% of genes ([Larkin et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth757), abstract only). RNA-seq detects low-abundance transcripts better and has a broader dynamic range than microarrays ([Zhao et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0078644)), and its advantage in qPCR-verified differential expression comes mainly from low-abundance genes ([Wang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.3001)); disagreement between an array and an RNA-seq dataset should therefore be expected to concentrate at low abundance. Distribution-matching transforms such as Training Distribution Matching and quantile normalization make array and RNA-seq data usable together for machine learning ([Thompson et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.1621)), but they do so by imposing a common distribution, which is why such transforms cannot serve as evidence of comparability.

Normalization units differ in what they assume. TPM is a relative molar concentration whose average is fixed by construction ([Wagner et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12064-012-0162-3), abstract only), so TPM depends on the composition of the RNA population and is not directly comparable across conditions or protocols that change that composition ([Zhao et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1261/rna.074922.120)). TMM seeks a stable reference after trimming extreme expression and fold-change values, with robustness to roughly 30% one-directional differential expression reported in the simulations of [Robinson and Oshlack 2010](https://doi.org/10.1186/gb-2010-11-3-r25); DESeq2 shares information across genes and shrinks fold changes because low-count log fold change estimates are noisy ([Love et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-014-0550-8)).

[Evans et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/bib/bbx008) distinguish assumptions by method: for an mRNA-per-cell target, library-size scaling requires constant total mRNA per cell, whereas distribution/testing methods rely on balanced expression and shared technical effects on DE and non-DE genes. Their simulations show why widespread asymmetric changes can bias normalization; a majority of unchanged genes is not a universal requirement, and sufficiently symmetric changes can preserve performance. The review explicitly notes that the assumptions change when the target is mRNA per transcriptome rather than mRNA per cell; controls also have assumptions of their own. These limits qualify the interpretation of normalized levels and responses. [Lovén et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2012.10.012) show with spike-in standards that a global increase in mRNA per cell is misread by normalizations that assume constant total output.

Within-sample ranks are invariant to monotone unit changes (counts to log2 counts, TPM to log TPM), which supports the repository's rank-based default, but ranks do not remove composition effects or gene-length effects that differ between units.

Proteomics adds missingness. Peptide lists from pairs of technical replicates overlapped by only 35–60% in an interlaboratory LC-MS/MS study ([Tabb et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1021/pr9006365)), and the proteomic protocol of [Čuklina et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.15252/msb.202110240) treats missing values and intensity drift as proteomics-specific problems. The set of proteins quantified in both datasets is therefore itself a variable, and a correlation computed on it is conditioned on detectability.

### Cyanobacterial compendia

Three cyanobacterial compendia were found, and all three compare studies on the response scale. CyanoEXpress normalized each Synechocystis sp. PCC 6803 dataset individually and computed log2 fold changes against each study's own controls, covering 3,073 genes and 178 perturbations ([Hernández-Prieto and Futschik 2012](https://doi.org/10.6026/97320630008634)). The subsequent meta-analysis of 756 microarray measurements from 37 studies retained 722 after curation, noted that the datasets came from different laboratories and different wild-type strains and that "normal" or "standard" conditions varied between experiments, and for that reason fitted linear models to each study's reference design to derive expression contrasts; it also found the photosynthetic apparatus subject to greater expression changes than other cellular components ([Hernández-Prieto et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep22168)). The *S. elongatus* PCC 7942 iModulon compendium reprocessed public reads through a standardized pipeline and normalized log-TPM to a reference condition specific to each project ([Yuan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410492121)). None of the three attempted to declare two studies' baseline conditions equivalent; each made the within-study contrast the unit of comparison. Gene-expression meta-analysis more generally gains reproducibility by adding datasets and applying effect-size thresholds and by modelling between-study heterogeneity rather than assuming it away ([Sweeney et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/nar/gkw797)). Co-expression module preservation statistics ([Langfelder et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1001057)) and integrative correlation ([Parmigiani et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-03-0490)) are compendium-scale tools: they need many samples per dataset and are not per-pair defaults here.

## Pitfalls with sources

1. **Matched marginal distributions are weak evidence.** Quantile normalization works by forcing the observed distributions to be the same ([Hicks and Irizarry 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-015-0679-0); [Bolstad et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/19.2.185), abstract only), distribution-matching transforms impose a target distribution deliberately ([Thompson et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.1621)), and global normalizations assume that observed global differences are technical, with the potential to remove biologically driven variation ([Hicks and Irizarry 2015](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-015-0679-0); [Lovén et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2012.10.012)). A KS test on two normalized datasets mostly tests whether they used the same normalization.
2. **Normalization unit changes the similarity score.** TPM depends on RNA composition ([Zhao et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1261/rna.074922.120); [Wagner et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12064-012-0162-3)); DESeq/TMM normalization depends on a stable or sufficiently balanced expression reference and can be biased by widespread asymmetric changes ([Evans et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/bib/bbx008); [Robinson and Oshlack 2010](https://doi.org/10.1186/gb-2010-11-3-r25); [Love et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-014-0550-8)); log2 array intensity has a narrower dynamic range ([Zhao et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0078644)). Comparing a TPM table with a DESeq2-normalized table measures the units as well as the biology.
3. **Platform depresses level agreement, mostly at low abundance.** Cross-platform correlation on identical samples is about 0.9, not replicate-level ([Zhao et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0078644)); laboratory effects are large even within one platform ([Irizarry et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth756)).
4. **Batch and condition are confounded across studies.** Clustering can track processing batch ([Leek et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrg2825); [Gilad and Mizrahi-Man 2015](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.6536.1)); batch correction in unbalanced or confounded designs deflates or invents group differences ([Nygaard et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxv027); [Goh et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tibtech.2017.02.012)).
5. **Correlation is not agreement.** Pearson and Spearman ignore offsets and scale ([Bland and Altman 1986](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2886%2990837-8); [Lin 1989](https://doi.org/10.2307/2532051)) and can be spurious on relative data ([Lovell et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004075)).
6. **Replicate count limits every metric.** With one replicate the yardstick does not exist, which is why the *S. elongatus* compendium filtered such samples ([Yuan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410492121)); with three replicates most tools recovered only 20–40% of the genes significant with 42, and at least six are recommended ([Schurch et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1261/rna.053959.115)). Response vectors from two- or three-replicate studies are noisy and should use shrunken fold changes ([Love et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-014-0550-8)).
7. **Strain shifts the meaning of a nominal condition.** UTEX 2973 and PCC 7942 differ by 55 SNPs and indels ([Yu et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep08132)), yet PCC 7942 grows best at 38 °C while UTEX 2973 has its optimum at 42 °C ([Ungerer et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1128/mBio.02327-17)), so one temperature sits at different points on the two strains' response curves. Cross-strain metrics also depend on the pangenome crosswalk, whose per-strain CDS coverage is recorded in `docs/validation/data-contract.md:159-174`; the join itself remains the repository's.
8. **Response agreement can be generic.** Recurrent differential expression predicts hit lists regardless of question ([Crow et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1802973116)); in Synechocystis the photosynthetic apparatus changes more than other components across perturbations ([Hernández-Prieto et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep22168)), so two unrelated stresses may agree through a shared photosynthesis response.
9. **Weak responses agree poorly even when matched.** Concordance scales with effect size ([Wang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1038/nbt.3001)).
10. **The shared-feature set is selected.** The reliable subset varies by platform and population ([Garrett-Mayer et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/biostatistics/kxm033)); proteomic identifications overlap 35–60% even between technical replicates ([Tabb et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1021/pr9006365)).

## Recommended metric set

All metrics are computed per pair of datasets within one data type, on uncorrected data, after converting each sample to within-sample ranks over the shared features (the repository's rank default). No metric is combined into a single score and none carries a pass mark; each is shown with its own yardstick or reference distribution. "Minimum data" is the least a dataset must provide for the metric to be computable at all; it is not a quality threshold, and where the literature offers no established minimum (for shared-gene count in particular) none is proposed.

| # | Metric | What it is | Can show | Cannot show | Minimum data | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M1 | Shared-feature coverage | Number of genes or proteins quantified in both datasets after the locus crosswalk, and the fraction of each dataset's features this represents; for proteomics, features present in at least half the replicates on each side | How much of the genome every other metric rests on; whether the overlap is skewed toward abundant features | Anything about condition | A per-gene table with identifiers the repository can map | Garrett-Mayer et al. 2007; Tabb et al. 2009 |
| M2 | Replicate yardstick | Distribution (median, range) of Spearman correlations between all pairs of biological replicates within each dataset, for each condition | The noise floor each dataset sets; low replicate agreement flags a dataset whose other metrics are uninformative | Accuracy; a dataset can replicate well and still be biased | ≥2 replicate-level profiles per condition; with 1, not computable | Li et al. 2011; Čuklina et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2024 |
| M3 | Level concordance on the yardstick | Spearman correlation between every cross-dataset sample pair, shown as a distribution beside the two M2 distributions; summary: the median cross-pair value and its position relative to the two replicate ranges; Lin's concordance correlation on mean ranks as a companion | Whether the two datasets are about as similar as replicates, modestly below, or far below | Which factor (condition, platform, lab, strain) causes a gap; a gap is expected even for identical biology across platforms | M1 plus M2 on both sides; single-sample datasets get the cross value without a yardstick | Irizarry et al. 2005; Zhao et al. 2014; Lin 1989; Bland and Altman 1986 |
| M4 | Agreement plot | Difference in mean rank against average mean rank per gene (Bland–Altman form), with the genes outside the replicate-derived band listed | Intensity-dependent disagreement (e.g. array compression at low abundance) and the specific genes driving a gap | A global verdict | As M3 | Bland and Altman 1986; Wang et al. 2014 |
| M5 | Response concordance | Where each dataset contains a contrast against its own control, Spearman correlation of the two shrunken log2 fold-change vectors on shared genes, sign agreement among each side's top-ranked genes, and an RRHO2 map; shown beside the response magnitude on each side and beside the distribution of the same statistic across unrelated contrast pairs in the data type | Whether the two studies perturbed the cells in the same direction; the strongest data-derived evidence of comparable conditions | Comparability of the baselines; specificity (generic stress responses agree); a low value is uninformative when both responses are small | Both datasets have a treatment and a matched control, each with ≥2 replicates | SEQC/MAQC-III 2014; Parmigiani et al. 2004; Plaisier et al. 2010; Cahill et al. 2018; Hernández-Prieto et al. 2016; Crow et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2014 |
| M6 | Joint embedding with known labels | PCA (and hierarchical clustering) of rank-transformed samples from the selected datasets, coloured by study and separately by nominal condition; where any condition occurs in more than one study, the fraction of variance explained by study versus condition | Whether samples group by study (batch-like) or by condition; whether the design permits the two to be separated at all | Separation when every study has a unique condition (study and condition are then confounded and the decomposition is not identifiable) | ≥2 datasets with replicates; ≥1 condition shared across studies for the variance split | Leek et al. 2010; Gilad and Mizrahi-Man 2015; Reese et al. 2013; Hoffman and Schadt 2016 |
| M7 | Marginal distribution check (display only) | Overlaid density or QQ plot of each dataset's values in its stated unit, with a KS statistic | Unit or scaling errors (wrong log base, unlogged data, a TPM table labelled as counts) | Biological comparability; matched distributions are largely an artefact of normalization | A per-gene table in a declared unit | Hicks and Irizarry 2015; Bolstad et al. 2003; Zhao et al. 2020 |

Two further metrics belong at compendium scale rather than per pair: module preservation statistics ([Langfelder et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1001057)) and integrative correlation ([Parmigiani et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1158/1078-0432.CCR-03-0490)) need many samples per dataset to estimate gene–gene correlation and are worth revisiting only if a uniformly reprocessed compendium of the size of [Yuan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410492121) is assembled.

The "other" group in the owner's instruction maps naturally onto M3 applied to control samples. When two studies each report a "standard" control culture, their control-versus-control level concordance on the M2 yardstick is the most direct data-derived measure of how standard their standard conditions were, which is exactly the variation the Synechocystis meta-analysis reported between experiments ([Hernández-Prieto et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep22168)).

## What the interface should show beside each metric

1. The number of shared features (M1) and the replicate count on each side, printed next to every value computed from them.
2. Each dataset's own replicate range (M2) as a band on the same axis as the cross-dataset value (M3), so the reader sees distance from the noise floor rather than an absolute correlation.
3. Platform, normalization unit as deposited, and whether values were reprocessed uniformly or taken from author tables, because pitfalls 2 and 3 explain much of any gap.
4. Strain on each side, with a marker when the pair crosses strains (pitfall 7).
5. For M5, the response magnitude on each side (for example the number of genes beyond a stated fold change) and where the pair's value falls in the distribution over unrelated contrast pairs, so that weak responses (pitfall 9) and generic responses (pitfall 8) are visible.
6. For M6, an explicit statement when study and condition are confounded and the variance split is therefore not computable.
7. A "not computable" state with its reason (single replicate, no control, no per-gene table) rather than a blank or a zero.
8. The package-D axis results for the same pair beside, not merged with, the data-derived metrics, together with the compact condition scales the owner described.
9. A note that batch-corrected values are not used for any comparability metric (pitfall 4).

## Fit to the current repository data

A keyword classification of the `per_gene_table` column of `docs/notes/handoff/cyano_package_B_conditions_20261003.tsv` (provisional: it matches substrings, not a curated field) finds that of 57 transcriptomics rows, 32 record no per-gene table, 17 mention counts and one mentions TPM; all 22 proteomics rows record no per-gene table. Most metrics above therefore become computable only after reprocessing from raw reads, as the *S. elongatus* compendium did with a standardized pipeline ([Yuan et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410492121)). Uniform reprocessing would also neutralise pitfall 2 within RNA-seq. Whether and how to reprocess is a repository decision under the admission contract. The current rule in `docs/validation/data-contract.md:135-157` requires every metadata axis to agree; the metrics here are proposed as evidence displayed alongside that rule for human review, consistent with the owner's 2026-10-04 instruction, and do not amend it.

## Open gaps

No source retrieved this session establishes a minimum shared-gene count, a numerical between-to-within ratio, or a response-correlation value at which two cross-study datasets should be called comparable; the literature reports these quantities relative to replicates and reference distributions, which is why the memo proposes display, not thresholds. No cross-study comparability analysis specific to *S. elongatus* proteomics was found. Ribosome-profiling and Tn-seq comparability were out of scope.
